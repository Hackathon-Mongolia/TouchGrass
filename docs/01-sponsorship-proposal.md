## 2. Why this hackathon

Ulaanbaatar is one of the few capitals where the environment is a direct, measurable cause of illness, and almost none of the city's young technical talent has been pointed at it.

- **Air.** On the coldest days, daily PM2.5 in Ulaanbaatar has reached 687 micrograms per cubic metre, 27 times the WHO guideline. Pneumonia is the second leading cause of death for Mongolian children under five, and admissions rise with the particle count. The 2025 annual average fell to 17.9 from 25.7 the year before, which shows the problem moves when the city acts.
- **Heat.** After raw coal was banned in 2019, acute carbon monoxide poisoning cases rose as much as 50-fold. A parliamentary working group counted 779 deaths from carbon monoxide between 2017 and 2024.
- **Ground and water.** The city's seven districts hold about 145,000 pit latrines. A study found 88 percent of ger-area soils bacterially contaminated and E. coli in a 47-metre well, and linked soil contamination to diarrhoeal disease in children under five. Heavy-metal risk to children exceeds permissible limits near industrial sites.
- **Waste.** The city produces thousands of tonnes of waste a day; about a fifth is recycled and 7 percent is illegally dumped, mostly in the ger areas with the fewest services.
- **Climate.** Mongolia has warmed 1.59 degrees since 2005, nearly three times the global rate. Six major dzuds in a decade; 8.1 million animals died in the winter of 2023 to 2024, with consequences for the health, income and mental health of a third of the population.

The data exists and is open: air-quality stations publish hourly, the National Statistics Office has an open API, health indicators are public. What is missing is people building on it. Mongolia has had hackathons on mining data, health tech and air pollution, run by the World Bank, IFC, UNICEF and the city, but none has been student-led, none has combined health and environment, and nothing independent has passed 150 participants since 2021. This is the first at this scale.
