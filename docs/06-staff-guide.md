# Touch Grass — Staff Guide
## What a hackathon is, how we are organized, and what you do before and during the event

For every organizer, team lead and volunteer of Touch Grass, the 24-hour youth hackathon on health and the environment, November 7–8, 2026. Mentors and judges have their own one-page briefs in sections 10 and 11. Read sections 1, 2, 3 and 7 today; read your team's part of section 4 this week; read sections 6 to 9 in the week before the event.

---

## 1. What a hackathon is

Most of us have run a Model United Nations conference. A hackathon has the same skeleton, registration, venue, delegates, food, ceremonies, sponsors, and a different heart. At a MUN the content is the debate, and delegates bring it. At a hackathon the content is 55 working prototypes, and participants build them in the room, overnight, under a clock. Our job is to make the building possible and the judging fair.

**The shape of it.** Participants arrive Saturday morning, form teams of two to four, and sign up for one of five tracks on a public board. At 13:00 a 24-hour clock starts. They build: software, hardware, data tools, whatever solves a real problem. We feed them five times, run two short workshops, send mentors to walk the tables, keep the building safe through the night, and count down the hours from the microphone. At 13:00 Sunday the clock stops and submissions close. Judges visit every table, score each team against a published rubric, the five track winners, and sometimes one wildcard, present on stage, and we hand out awards at 16:20.

**The vocabulary** is in section 13. The six words you need today: a **track** is one of five areas (Healthcare, Sustainability, Education, Humanity, AI); there is no problem list, so each team brings its own idea; a **prototype** is anything that works, however roughly; a **demo** is showing it running; the **rubric** is the scoring sheet every judge uses; **submission** is the project page a team files before the deadline.

**What success looks like for staff, in order:** nobody is hurt or lost; everyone is fed on time; every team that submitted gets judged; judging finishes on time; sponsors receive exactly what they were promised; the room is handed back clean.

**What you are not here to do:** solve participants' problems for them, judge their ideas, promise anything to a sponsor, or decide anything alone that section 3 says is somebody else's.

## 3. How we are organized

**Two organizations.** Hackathon Mongolia is us: a student collective, not a registered entity, so we never sign contracts or touch money. Uram Enerel NGO signs, receives, pays, keeps receipts and reports, and brings its school chapters and health network.

**Two main organizers decide.** [Co-organizer] leads sponsor and partner relationships and shares the stage. Anar leads the program, the documents, and judging, and is MC. If a question is not answered by this guide or the timeline, ask them; they answer the same day.

**Four teams execute.** Logistics, finance, marketing, operations. Each has a lead. Section 4 says what each owns.

**One adult lead** is legally responsible on site for the whole 24 hours and on call overnight. Anything involving a participant's safety, a parent, the venue's management or the police goes to the adult lead first.

**How we talk.** One organizer chat. Team leads post a three-line update every Wednesday: done, next, blocked. All-hands every Sunday evening for 30 minutes: October 11, 18, 25, November 1. Decisions are made in those two places or by the main organizers the same day. On the event days, one radio or phone group for staff; participants are in a separate chat.

**Where things are.** Every document is in the GitHub repository. The timeline is the source of truth for who does what by when.

## 7. How to behave with participants

- **You are a host, not a supervisor.** Participants chose to spend their weekend here. Greet, help, step back.
- **Answer the question or find who can.** The three answers you may give: the fact, "the organizer desk knows," or "I will find out and come back." Never guess about rules, prizes, judging or safety.
- **A stuck team gets a mentor, not your code.** Point them to the mentor desk. Mentors explain and debug; they do not build.
- **No favorites.** You do not score, you do not hint at scores, you do not tell a team what judges like.
- **Promise nothing.** Not a prize, not a sponsor introduction, not an exception to a deadline. Those are the main organizers' calls.
- **Minors are minors.** Under-18s are tagged at check-in. They leave the building only through the sign-out log with a parent, and they are the first people the night shift counts.
- **Conflicts.** Separate, listen, de-escalate. If it does not settle in two minutes, call an organizer. Code-of-conduct breaches, harassment, alcohol, drugs, smoking: organizer immediately, adult lead immediately, and the participant is removed after the parent is called.
- **Sponsors.** Be warm. If a sponsor asks for anything outside what they were promised, the answer is "let me get [co-organizer]." Never say yes or no yourself.

## 8. Safety and the overnight

- **The adult lead** is reachable at [phone] for the full 24 hours and makes every safety call.
- **Consent forms** for every under-18 are at the organizer desk with emergency contacts, medical notes and the overnight choice. The check-in tag shows who may stay and who leaves at 21:00.
- **Sign-out window 21:00 Saturday.** Participants going home leave with their pickup and sign the log. From 22:00 to 07:00 nobody leaves the building without a parent at the door, logged.
- **Night shift:** two adults awake at all times, one at the desk, one walking the floor every 30 minutes and marking the roster. Quiet hours 01:00 to 06:00: no music, no microphone. Sleeping rooms are single-gender, each with an adult volunteer of the same gender; participants bring their own sleeping bag and mat.
- **Illness or injury:** first aider at the organizer desk; adult lead decides on calling 103 and the parent; a staff member stays with the participant until a parent or the ambulance arrives.
- **Missing participant:** check the sign-out log, the sleeping rooms and the bathrooms, in that order; after ten minutes, adult lead calls the parent.
- **Fire or evacuation:** follow the venue's plan, learned at the Friday setup; floor walkers clear tables; headcount at the assembly point against the check-in list.
- **Security** from the venue or our contractor is at the door overnight and has the adult lead's number.

## 9. Judging day

- **11:00** judges arrive; operations briefs them for 30 minutes in the side room: rubric, their track's eleven teams, timing, conflicts of interest.
- **13:00** the submission form closes. No exceptions, announced from the stage. Tables stay as they are.
- **13:30–15:00** five panels of three judges visit their track's teams: three minutes of demo, two of questions. A timekeeper per panel keeps the five-minute slot with a phone timer and a hand signal at 30 seconds. Judges score on paper independently.
- **15:00** two staff tally independently and compare; the five or six finalists, five track winners plus at most one wildcard, are read from the stage and posted in the chat.
- **15:15–16:00** finals: eight teams, five minutes each, full panel scoring. Stage tech resets between teams.
- **16:00** judges confer in the side room for fifteen minutes. On stage, sponsor thanks.
- **16:20** awards in this order: Most Creative, Most Impactful, Most Technically Complex, five track awards presented by track sponsors, then third, second, first, with the first prize presented by the title sponsor. Certificates are filled in during the finals; prize envelopes or products are lined up on the stage table in order.
- **16:50** group photo with sponsor boards in frame; survey link on the screen.

## 10. Mentor brief

You are here to help teams think and unblock, not to build. Walk your track's tables during mentor hours, Saturday 16:00 and Sunday 10:00, and whenever a team flags you. Ask what they are trying to do before you look at their screen. Debug with them, explain the concept, point to the documentation, then leave; the next team is waiting. Do not write their code, pick their idea, or tell them what judges want. Night mentors are on call in the chat from 23:00 to 07:00 and answer within fifteen minutes. If a team is in distress rather than stuck, tell the organizer desk.

---

## 11. Judge brief

Arrive Sunday 11:00 for the briefing. You judge the eleven teams in your track with two co-judges; a timekeeper keeps each visit to five minutes. Score every criterion from 1 to 5 on your own sheet before discussing with your co-judges; the weights are on the sheet: solves the stated problem for the stated user 20 percent, technical difficulty 20, use of evidence and data 15, completeness of the demo 15, design and usability 15, creativity 15. Judge what runs in front of you, not the slides. Ask one hard question per team. You do not score a team from your own company or school; tell the timekeeper and your co-judges score it. In the finals the full panel scores all eight on the same rubric. Ties: three minutes of discussion, then the higher score on "solves the stated problem" wins. What you say to a team at its table should leave it better than you found it.

## 13. Glossary

- **Hackathon** — a timed competition where teams build working prototypes.
- **Hack** — to build something quickly that works; nothing to do with breaking into systems.
- **Track** — one of the five areas; each team competes within one.
- **Track board** — the public board where each team writes its name under the track it entered.
- **Prototype** — the thing a team builds; it works, roughly, and proves the idea.
- **MVP** — minimum viable product; the smallest version that works end to end.
- **Demo** — showing the prototype running, live, in three minutes.
- **Pitch** — the spoken explanation of the problem, the solution and who it is for.
- **Submission** — the project page a team files before the deadline: description, demo video or screenshots, repository link, tools used.
- **Repository, repo** — where a team's code lives online; judges may look at its history to confirm the work was done during the event.
- **Rubric** — the scoring sheet with six criteria and weights; every judge uses the same one.
- **Expo judging** — judges come to the tables, rather than teams to a stage.
- **Finals** — the eight best teams presenting on stage for the overall top three.
- **Mentor** — an experienced helper who explains and debugs but does not build.
- **Judge** — scores teams; three per track.
- **Time check** — an announcement of hours remaining, from the stage and in the chat.
- **Quiet hours** — 01:00 to 06:00, no music or microphone; sleeping rooms open.
- **Code of conduct** — the rules of behavior everyone agreed to at registration.
- **Consent form** — signed by a parent for every under-18; covers participation, the overnight, emergencies and photos.
- **Sign-out log** — the book at the desk recording every minor who leaves with a parent.
- **Run-of-show** — the minute-by-minute schedule with who is on the microphone.
- **API** — a way for software to fetch data from a service; several tracks use public ones.
- **Open data** — datasets anyone may use; air-quality stations, the statistics office and health indicators publish them.
- **Wildcard** — a finalist slot given to a high-scoring team that did not win its track.

## 14. Staff FAQ

**I have never been to a hackathon. Is that a problem?** No. Your MUN experience covers the venue, the people, the food and the ceremonies, which is most of the work. The hackathon-specific parts, judging, mentors, the track board, belong to operations, and this guide plus the Friday briefing is enough.

**A participant asks me whether their idea is good.** Say you are not a judge and send them to a mentor.

**A team wants five minutes past the deadline.** No. Deadlines are the fairness of the whole event. Say so kindly and point at the clock.

**A sponsor wants a list of participants.** "Let me get [co-organizer]." Only opt-in CVs are shared, after the event.

**Someone's parent arrives at 2 a.m.** Adult lead to the door; sign the participant out; done.

**The wifi is down.** Logistics lead, then the venue's contact, then the backup line; announce from the stage that hotspots are allowed meanwhile.

**I am exhausted.** Tell your team lead. Nobody works more than 14 hours; the roster exists to be used.
